    Nearsighted vs farsighted: what your prescription means

    Look at the sign in front of the SPH number. A minus (−) means nearsighted: far things look blurry. A plus (+) means farsighted: close things look blurry. The bigger the number, the stronger the lens. An ADD is extra power for reading, common after 40, and it is not the same as being farsighted.

    Choose a frameHow to read your prescription

    Nearsighted (myopia)

    Far-away objects look blurry because the shape of the eye makes light focus in front of the retina instead of on it, the National Eye Institute (NEI) explains. Other signs are squinting and eye strain. It usually starts between ages 6 and 14 and gets worse until the early twenties.

    On a prescription: a minus SPH, like −1.25 or −4.00. The lens is thinnest in the center and thicker at the edge.

    Farsighted (hyperopia)

    Nearby objects look blurry because light focuses behind the retina, when the eyeball is too short from front to back or the cornea or the lens has a different shape (NEI). Common symptoms are trouble seeing up close, eye strain and headaches, especially when reading. Mild farsightedness may cause no symptoms at all.

    On a prescription: a plus SPH, like +1.00 or +3.25. The lens is thicker in the center.

    Presbyopia and the ADD

    Presbyopia is a normal part of aging, not farsightedness: the lens inside the eye gets harder and less flexible and stops focusing up close (NEI). You may start to notice it shortly after 40, the American Academy of Ophthalmology says, often by holding your phone or a menu farther away. Everyone gets it with age, whether nearsighted, farsighted or neither.

    On a prescription it appears as an ADD: a plus number, usually between +0.75 and +3.00, often written once for both eyes. With an ADD, progressive lenses give you far and near in one pair; for reading only, your eye doctor can write a near prescription for single vision lenses.

    Reading real examples

    OD −2.25 · OS −2.00: both eyes nearsighted, the right one a little more.

    OD +1.50 · OS +1.75: both eyes farsighted.

    OD −0.75 · OS +0.50: one eye of each kind. One pair of glasses corrects both.

    SPH −3.00 · CYL −1.00 · AXIS 90 · ADD +2.00: nearsighted, with astigmatism and a reading addition: a typical prescription for progressive lenses.

    PL or plano: 0.00, no nearsighted or farsighted correction in that eye.

    Which lenses

    No ADD: single vision lenses, one power for the distance your prescription is written for. With an ADD: progressive lenses, or office lenses for long hours at a desk. Strong prescriptions on either side of zero stay thin with high-index lenses. Not sure you copied yours right? Check your numbers and their signs before you order.

    Questions

    Good to know

    Is −2.00 nearsighted or farsighted?
    Nearsighted. Any minus SPH is nearsighted and any plus SPH is farsighted; the size of the number is the strength of the lens.
    Can one eye be nearsighted and the other farsighted?
    Yes. Each eye has its own numbers, and one pair of glasses corrects both. More often, a nearsighted person also gets presbyopia after 40 and needs help far and near: that is what the ADD and progressive lenses are for.
    What does ADD mean on a prescription?
    Extra magnifying power for reading. It is always a plus number, usually between +0.75 and +3.00, often written once for both eyes, and common after 40.
    Do nearsighted and farsighted lenses look different?
    Yes. A minus lens is thinnest in the center and thicker at the edge; a plus lens is thicker in the center. With a strong prescription, high-index lenses and a smaller frame keep either one thinner.
