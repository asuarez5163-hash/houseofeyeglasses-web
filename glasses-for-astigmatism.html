    Glasses for astigmatism: lenses and frames that work

    Regular prescription glasses correct astigmatism. The CYL and AXIS numbers on your prescription tell the lab how much correction to add and in which direction, and any single vision or progressive lens can carry it. What matters most is an exact prescription, a frame that fits and sits level, and a thin lens if your numbers are high.

    What astigmatism is

    Astigmatism happens when the cornea (the clear front of the eye) or the lens inside it has a different shape than normal, the National Eye Institute (NEI) explains: more like a football than a basketball, in the American Academy of Ophthalmology’s words. Light doesn’t focus evenly, so things look blurry or distorted, near and far. Other common symptoms are squinting, eye strain, headaches and trouble seeing at night.

    It is very common. The AAO says most people have 0.5 to 0.75 diopters of astigmatism, and people with 1.5 or more typically need glasses or contacts to see clearly. Only an eye exam can tell how much you have.

    The numbers: CYL and AXIS

    CYL (cylinder) is the amount of astigmatism correction, in diopters. Blank or 0.00 means none. Doctors may write it with a minus or a plus sign: both describe the same correction, and the lab works with either.

    AXIS is the direction of that correction, a number from 1 to 180 degrees. It is a direction, not a strength: 180 is not stronger than 90. It only appears next to a CYL value.

    Example: OD −1.50 −0.75 × 180 means SPH −1.50, CYL −0.75, AXIS 180. How to read your prescription has a free checker that spells out every sign.

    Which lenses

    Single vision lenses if your prescription has no ADD; progressive lenses if it has one and you want far and near in one pair. Both carry your CYL and AXIS.

    CYL adds power in one direction, so it counts toward how thick the lens gets: the material we recommend in the last step adds SPH and CYL together. Up to about ±4 in total, the polycarbonate included with every pair is thin enough; from about ±4, high-index 1.67 or 1.74 keeps the lens thin. An anti-reflective coating takes reflections off the lens surfaces.

    Frames that work

    Any frame can take astigmatism lenses; fit matters more than style. Because the correction has a direction, the lab sets it at your AXIS, and a frame that sits level and doesn’t slip keeps it where your eye doctor measured it. Choose one that rests evenly on your nose and ears, about as wide as your face, with your eyes near the center of each lens: find your frame size with the numbers on the glasses you own.

    With a strong prescription, a smaller, rounder lens and a full rim keep the thick edges less visible.

    New glasses and night driving

    A new or changed prescription can take a little getting used to. The AAO notes that mild eye strain, headaches or a sense of blur can happen with a new prescription, particularly in the first week; if they last longer, have the prescription rechecked.

    Trouble seeing at night is one of the symptoms of astigmatism (NEI). The right prescription is what corrects it; an anti-reflective coating helps by keeping the lenses themselves from adding reflections from headlights and street lamps.

      Questions

      Good to know

      Can glasses correct astigmatism?
      Yes. Glasses and contact lenses are the most common treatments for astigmatism, the National Eye Institute says. For severe or irregular astigmatism, the AAO notes rigid gas permeable contact lenses may work best; your eye doctor will tell you what suits your eyes.
      Do I need special frames for astigmatism?
      No. Any frame we carry can take astigmatism lenses. What matters is a good fit: a frame that sits level and stays in place, so the correction stays where it was measured.
      Is astigmatism the same as nearsightedness?
      No, though they often come together. Nearsightedness blurs things far away; astigmatism can blur near and far. One prescription corrects both: SPH for nearsightedness or farsightedness, CYL and AXIS for astigmatism.
      Do high-index lenses work with astigmatism?
      Yes. 1.67 and 1.74 carry your CYL and AXIS like any other lens. They are worth it when SPH and CYL together go past about ±4.
